01

What AI Is, and Isn't

AI stands for artificial intelligence. Let's start on the same page before we go anywhere else.

So what is AI, really?

Tools like ChatGPT or Gemini are called large language models. That means they've read a huge amount of text. They're very good at predicting the next likely word.

There's no understanding behind the reply. No memory of who you are. No inner life. Just very good pattern-matching, dressed up in confident-sounding sentences.

It can sound thoughtful without being right. Fluency and accuracy are two very different things.

Four things people get wrong

“It's sentient.”

It has no beliefs, feelings, or self-awareness. Just probability, applied at a massive scale.

“If it sounds confident, it's right.”

Tone carries zero information about accuracy. Confident and wrong happen together more often than you'd think.

“It's neutral.”

It reflects whatever patterns, and biases, were in its training text. That's not the same as objective truth.

“It remembers me.”

Most tools don't actually carry a relationship with you between chats, unless you've told them to.

Dig deeper: how it works inside

Three short explainers on what's going on under the hood.

Words become points

Each word is stored as a vector: a point in multidimensional space. Example: key words like queen, power, and female help it predict more words.

More dimensions, more power

Graphing those points in different spaces helps it calculate more outcomes. More dimensions make it more powerful.

Culture shapes meaning

Because of a nation's culture and history, a word could sit next to something different, instead of just following English.

Odd sentences throw it off

An unusual sentence carries a word and its subject to a different graph or dimension, which can completely throw off the LLM.

Inside the model, every word uses these three to decide what to pay attention to.

Query

Each word asks a question about the words around it. In “the tall tower,” tower asks: which words describe me?

Keys

Determine the matches, and how much influence each one exerts based on how close the match is.

Values

Carry the content that gets passed on: the push that moves a word in a new direction in vector space.

  1. Start with random numbers

    Every embedding begins as totally random numbers.

  2. Hide the last word

    Take a bit of training data: a sentence or paragraph that's missing its last word.

  3. Make a prediction

    Run the transforming procedure, turning the second-to-last word into a guess for the next one.

Then repeat

Check how far off the prediction was, then do the same thing with billions of other pieces. It keeps transforming words until the predicted word is the right one.

02

Using AI Safely & Effectively

How to get real value out of it, without getting burned.

How to actually use it

  1. Open the tool

    Go to the app or website, like ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude, and start a new chat.

  2. Type your question plainly

    Write like you are talking to a person. No special commands needed.

  3. Read it critically

    Ask yourself if it actually makes sense. Don't accept it just because it sounds sure of itself.

  4. Ask a follow-up

    If it's unclear or wrong, say so and ask again. That back-and-forth is normal, and it's how you get a better answer.

Where it's actually useful

Recipe helper

Tell it what's in your fridge and any food restrictions, it'll get creative. Works for allergies too.

Book recommendations

Describe a plot, mood, or vibe you're after, and it'll suggest a few matching reads.

Troubleshooting

Snap a photo of an error message or a strange part, and it'll point you toward what's going on.

Finding patterns

Feed it a few documents and ask where they agree or disagree.

One more: paste in a page full of ads and ask for a clean summary. It'll skip the clutter and just give you the content.

How much should you trust this?

Not every answer deserves the same amount of double-checking.

Higher trustLower trust

Stable, settled facts: dates, definitions, things with one clear answer.

  • “What's the capital of the United States?”
  • “Explain how photosynthesis works.”

Anything recent, contested, or personal: medical, legal, financial, or where real experts disagree.

  • “What's in the news today?”
  • “Is this safe with my medication?”

A few ground rules

Assume it's stored

What you type may be saved, reviewed, or used to train future versions.

Protect other people too

Never paste in someone else's private information, not just your own. You never know what is being used as training data.

Watch the confident tone

A friendly, certain voice isn't evidence. It's a design choice. It's built to make you feel good.

Verify before you act

For anything that matters, check it against a real, independent source. AI may be human backed, but it's not an actual human.

Dig deeper: getting better at it

Practical habits and the activities from the workshop.

A flat tire, a strange noise, a form you don't understand. AI can be a calm first stop when you're stressed and not sure what to do next.

Ask it a question, or show it a photo. It won't panic, and it can help you think through next steps. It won't always be right, but it can help you feel less stuck while you figure out the real answer.

Try this: show it a photo, or describe the problem, and ask: “what should I check first?” It's a starting point, not the final word. Use it to get calm, then verify anything that matters.

Watch out: the lines can blur, and you might switch roles mid-task.

The maker

Does the thing. The one who actually produces the work.

The coach

Teaches and gives feedback. A human coach already shares your context: culture, experience, your goals.

Coach the coach

AI starts from nothing. Tell it the audience, the goal, and the kind of feedback you want, more explicitly than you'd brief a friend.

Text, images, and video are all getting harder to tell apart from the real thing. A few tells still hold up:

That “too smooth” feeling

Generic phrasing, oddly perfect grammar, or a lack of specific, personal detail.

Look closely at the image

Odd hands, mismatched shadows, garbled background text, too-perfect skin, extra fingers.

Trace it back

Reverse image search, or search a distinctive phrase in quotes to find the source.

Check who's saying it

Who posted it, and do they have a track record? A confident caption isn't proof. Look for sources, and actually click that link.

This is called prompt engineering: choosing your words to get a better answer. Same AI tool, same topic, two different prompts. See how much the wording changes the answer.

  1. Pair up

    Both of you write down the same request on the same AI tool, e.g. “what laptop should i buy?”

  2. Prompt it your way

    Each of you rewrites it in your own words. Same AI, same topic, add detail, tone, or context.

  3. Compare notes

    Run both. Whose answer felt more useful, and why?

03

Where It Can Go Wrong

An honest look, and what actually helps.

Four categories of risk

Output risks

When AI just gets it wrong. It makes things up and invents facts, confidently, with no signal it's guessing. Links may not exist, citations may be fake, and sources may say the opposite.

Psychological risks

What it does to your mind over time.

Integrity risks

Passing off AI work as your own.

Societal risks

What it means for everyone: bias, jobs, ownership, and energy.

Yes, it makes things up

This is called “hallucination.” It sounds just as confident either way, and there's no built-in signal that it's guessing.

Fake citations

A real-sounding author, journal, and page number, for a study that doesn't exist.

Invented facts

Names, dates, and numbers stated as fact, with nothing behind them.

Broken or fake links

A link that goes nowhere, or to a page saying something else entirely.

The real skill isn't spotting what's good. It's knowing when, and how, to check.

Confidence isn't proof

AI sounds exactly as sure when it's wrong as when it's right. Can you tell these two apart?

“The treaty was signed in 1848.”

Correct, stated with total confidence.

“The treaty was signed in 1852.”

Wrong, stated with the exact same confidence.

Takeaway: use what the topic is, not how it sounds, to decide how hard to verify.

Dig deeper: the bigger costs

What overuse does to you, and what AI means for everyone else.

Losing skills

Overuse can make you forget how to do things, and it's hard to get those skills back.

Weaker judgment

Someone with no independent judgment isn't just worse at thinking. They're easier to steer.

Academic integrity

Turning in AI-generated work as your own: an essay, a problem set, code.

Who wants to steer you

Advertisers, politicians, scammers, and the AI and platform companies themselves. They want your money, your vote, your attention, your data, and your subscription.

Writing

If AI drafts everything for you, your own voice and ability to write under pressure can fade.

Navigation & memory

Leaning on GPS or reminders for everything can dull your sense of direction and recall.

Math & estimating

Always reaching for a calculator or an AI answer means you lose the habit of a rough mental check.

Judgment calls

Letting AI decide for you, again and again, is practice you're not getting.

The fix isn't avoiding AI. It's noticing when you lean on it out of habit, not need.

Who owns the work?

Models train on other people's writing and art, raising real questions about credit and consent.

Jobs and skills

Tasks inside jobs are changing fast, even where whole jobs aren't disappearing.

Bias at scale

Whatever bias exists in the training data can get repeated, to millions of people at once.

Leaning on it too much

Skills like writing and judgment can quietly erode if we outsource them by default.

Training a model happens once. Answering everyone's questions, every day, forever, doesn't. That part grows with use.

Electricity

Every question you ask uses power. Data centers run around the clock to keep up.

Water

Those same data centers need water to stay cool, especially in hot climates.

Hardware

The chips inside carry their own manufacturing footprint, before they even run a single query.

Exact figures vary by model and provider.

04

AI at Work & in Everyday Life

What's actually changing, and how to stay adaptable through it.

Jobs aren't vanishing, but tasks are shifting

That's the honest, unglamorous version of what's happening inside most workplaces right now.

What's shifting

Repetitive drafting, summarizing, and first-pass research, increasingly AI-assisted.

What's holding steady

Trust, relationships, judgment calls, and hands-on skill. Still hard for AI to fake.

What matters now

Knowing how to work with AI, and when not to, is becoming its own skill.

Chances are, you've already used it

Accessibility

Reading text aloud, describing images, and simplifying dense language.

Staying connected

Drafting messages, translating, and bridging generational or language gaps.

Learning & homework

Explaining a concept a different way when the textbook version isn't clicking.

Right here at the library

Search help, form navigation: the everyday questions you already bring to the desk.

Myth or fact?

Pick an answer for each statement to see why.

  1. 1AI understands what I'm asking it.

    Myth. AI does not truly understand your words. It just guesses the next best word based on math and patterns from its training data. It has no real mind, feelings, or true grasp of meaning.

  2. 2AI can sound completely certain and still be wrong.

    Fact. AI models often generate wrong information with a very confident and polite tone. This is called a hallucination.

  3. 3Everything an AI tells you has already been fact-checked.

    Myth. AI models generate text based on patterns in data, not by verifying every single fact against a live source.

  4. 4AI remembers you the same way a person would.

    Myth. A person remembers life through feelings, time, and a living brain. An AI only stores data in numbers and text files. It does not feel or live through past events.

  5. 5AI can help you draft, brainstorm, or summarize, as a starting point.

    Fact. Used as a starting point, it can help you draft, brainstorm, or summarize.

You don't have to get this perfect

Start small

Try it on something low-stakes first, a recipe or a packing list, not a big decision.

It's OK to say no

You don't have to use AI for everything, or ever, if you don't want to.

Mistakes are normal

You will get a wrong answer sometimes. That's why we double-check, not a sign you're doing it wrong.

You know more than you think

Everything here, you can practice on your own time, at your own pace.

Dig deeper: try it yourself

The closing activity from the workshop.

  1. We ask, on screen

    We type “give me a book recommendation about ___” into AI, live, so you can see exactly what we type.

  2. You try it

    Use your phone, or pair with a neighbor. Ask for a recommendation on a topic or mood you like.

  3. Bring it to the desk

    We check together: is that book real? Does it match what the AI said?

If you remember three things

  1. Match your trust to the topic

    Settled facts vs. recent, personal, or contested. Verify accordingly.

  2. Confidence isn't proof

    Tone tells you nothing about accuracy. Check the source, not the delivery.

  3. Protect what's yours

    Your data, your skills, your judgment. Use it as a tool, not a replacement.
